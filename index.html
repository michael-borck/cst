Cognitive strategy transfer

Why human management skills are the secret to better AI

The short version

The prompting techniques that work best are old human strategies. Show your working. Write a clear brief. Break the job into steps. Check the result against a standard.

A language model has read about all of them, so it can follow their form. It can't supply their substance. You bring the goal, the context and, above all, a check from outside the model. That is why people who already manage complex work well tend to get the most out of AI.

This page goes with my research poster. It adds a worked example, a checklist you can use tomorrow, and linked references. Superscript numbers match the poster.

The problem
52 citations. 2 real.
The form of a citation, nothing behind itA citation we could verify

We asked a frontier model to draft a literature review. It gave us 52 confident citations. We could verify two.

Then we asked it to check its own references. It confirmed the fakes.

Every fake had the form of scholarship: authors, journal, volume, DOI. It lacked the substance, a real paper to point to. Asking the model again only produced more form. Only a check from outside the model, looking each reference up in a bibliographic database, told the real ones from the fakes.

Not a one-off
  • 2023. A New York court sanctioned two lawyers whose brief cited six court decisions that didn't exist.1
  • 2025. Deloitte part-refunded the Australian Government for an A$440,000 report with invented sources.2
  • 2025. In a KPMG report, 40 of 45 reference titles turned out to be fabricated.3

02
The mechanism Answers RQ1

The model supplies the form. You supply the substance.

There are two usual explanations for why these strategies work, and they seem to pull against each other.

Similarity says models are trained on human text, so the structures of careful human reasoning steer them toward careful-looking output. Deficit says the strategies work because they cover for things models can't reliably do. The paper argues these are one mechanism seen from two ends.

Form

from the model

Strategies are written all through the training text as worked examples, specs and checklists, so the model can follow one when asked.

It produces the shape of reasoning without the reliability that shape signals in a person.10

+

Substance

from you, outside the model
TriggeringYou invoke the strategy. This is the weakest of the three. Models are tuned to answer straight away, so part of it is just asking.
GroundingThe goal, constraints and context of this particular task. They aren't in the model waiting to be triggered.
JudgmentA check against a standard outside the model. This is where most of the value is.
=

Actualised

a strategy that works

A student eventually internalises a strategy and stops needing the prompt. The model never does. You supply the substance again, every time.

A model can describe "check your work" fluently. It won't apply it unless someone imposes it from outside. In affordance terms, the model offers a possibility for action. Only the user can actualise it.4,5

Cao et al. wrapped a current frontier model in external monitoring and orchestration. The weights didn't change. Pooled accuracy on hard reasoning, coding and visual benchmarks went from 48.3 to 56.9.11

What it looks like in practice

Same model, same task, different substance

Say you need a one-page summary of this quarter's customer complaints for the leadership team.

Form only
Summarise our customer complaints this quarter and recommend three actions. Think step by step.

You'll get something fluent and well organised. It may invent trends, round numbers it never counted, and recommend things that sound like every other complaints report. "Think step by step" triggers the form of analysis. Nothing grounds it in your data or checks the result.

Form plus substance Grounding
Attached is the complaints export for July to September, 412 rows. The readers are the leadership team. They'll use this to decide where two extra support staff go next quarter. Use the categories in column D. Don't estimate anything that isn't in the file.
Triggering
Work in steps. Count by category. Compare with last quarter's counts on the second sheet. Then pick the three biggest changes and suggest one action for each.
Judgment
This part happens outside the model. Check the category counts with a pivot table. Ask the team lead who handles complaints whether the top three match what they see.

Typing "are you sure?" isn't a check. The model runs it through the same process that made the error, which is exactly what happened with the 52 citations. For references, the outside check is looking each one up in the library catalogue or at doi.org.

03
The boundaries Answers RQ2

Transfer holds as far as outside supply can reach

The framework makes a testable claim. A strategy helps when you can supply the missing substance from outside the model. It helps less as that gets harder, and fails where nothing outside can supply it.

Robust: supplied from outsideWeak: model checks itselfFails: needs novelty
Verification boundary

Outside checks beat self-checks

A model checking its own work runs the check through the process that made the error.10,12 A loop that critiques its own output reaches a wrong answer faster, and with more apparent authority. A loop that checks against something external, such as a test suite, a source or a reviewer, keeps its strength. The loop changes who pulls the trigger. It doesn't change where the standard lives.

One scope note. Models that are specifically trained to verify can move part of the check inside the model. The framework expects that kind of shift as models improve. It doesn't make "are you sure?" a check.

Expertise condition

Experts gain most where checks need know-how

Experts don't win by knowing AI tricks. They bring more of the substance, above all the judgment to catch a convincing wrong answer.13 So the prediction is specific. The expert advantage should be largest where checking needs domain knowledge, and smallest where a cheap outside check exists. Give a novice a test suite or an answer key and the gap narrows.

Hard outer limit

No prompt manufactures novelty

Models interpolate within their training data and extrapolate past it unreliably.14 If that holds, genuinely new ideas stay out of reach whatever you put in the prompt.

This is the most contested claim in the framework. The paper treats it as a working assumption, not a settled result.

The skills that make AI work are already in your workforce.

IS theory

Explains why prompting works without claiming the machine thinks. Effective AI use is affordance actualisation: the user turns what the tool offers into a result.

Organisations

Briefing, decomposing, specifying and reviewing already exist in your people, though rarely labelled as AI skills. Pair AI with domain experts rather than expecting it to replace them.

AI literacy

Teach prompting as applied management, not a list of incantations. Above all, teach external verification, and treat the model's confidence as telling you nothing.

A decision the framework changes

A firm adopting an AI coding assistant can fund prompt-engineering training for its developers, or an automated test suite and review workflow around the assistant's output. Most fund the training. The framework predicts the test suite wins, because it is a cheap outside check, and that the gap is largest for junior developers, who can least verify the output themselves.

For open-ended analytical work, where no such check exists, it predicts the opposite: expert judgment keeps the advantage. Both halves can be tested.

Try it tomorrow

Manage the model like a capable new colleague

  1. Write the brief you'd give a new starter.The goal, who it's for, the constraints, and what "done" looks like.
  2. Give it the material.Paste in the data, the policy or the sources. Don't ask it to recall them.
  3. Break the job into steps you can check one at a time.A long single request hides where it went wrong.
  4. Decide how you'll check the result before you read it.Once a fluent answer is in front of you, it's easy to find it convincing.
  5. Check against something outside the model.A source, a calculation, a test, a colleague. "Are you sure?" doesn't count.
  6. If you can't check it, don't rely on it.Bring in someone who can check it, or use the model for a part you can check.

What comes next

The framework makes predictions. These studies test them:

  • Experiment. Vary the strategy and the model's capability. Compare self-checks with outside checks, and see whether the gap widens for weaker models.
  • Design. Build and evaluate a professional-development programme that starts from the skills staff already have, instead of teaching AI as something new.
  • Teaching. Compare students taught prompting as applied management with students taught conventional prompt engineering, on tasks neither group has seen.
